Lakhani's 791-professional study,
translated for a 12-person business.

Harvard's Cybernetic Teammate paper is the single most important AI research published in the last two years for small and mid-sized businesses. Here's what it actually proved — and what to do about it on Monday morning.

The study, in plain language

In 2025, a team led by Professor Karim Lakhani at Harvard Business School — with Fabrizio Dell'Acqua and co-authors — ran a field experiment with 791 working professionals at Procter & Gamble. They had professionals tackle real new-product-development challenges in four conditions: individuals without AI, individuals with AI, teams of two without AI, and teams of two with AI.

Then they measured what each group produced: quality, novelty, breakthrough-ness. They had expert judges evaluate the work blind. They controlled for time on task.

The finding made the rounds in HBR and most business press, but the version that traveled was sometimes oversimplified. Here's the precise version:

Lakhani et al. · HBS · 2025 · Cybernetic Teammate
"Individual professionals working with AI matched the performance of two-person teams working without AI on breakthrough innovation output. The augmented individual is not a smaller team — it is a different operating unit with capacity comparable to a multi-person team on certain categories of knowledge work."

Three big numbers from the study:

791
P&G professionals in the field experiment
~1×
Individual + AI matched 2-person team without AI on breakthrough output
+12%
Average quality lift on top of baseline for AI-augmented groups

What this actually means

The temptation reading this study is to say "AI replaces teams." That's not quite right. Teams do other things — coordinate, lead, support each other emotionally, divide up administrative load, form culture. None of that goes away.

What changes is the unit of capacity. For decades, the assumption inside professional services and knowledge work has been: capacity scales with people. Need more output? Hire more. Need broader skills? Hire more. The whole structure of consulting firms — partner + manager + associate pyramids — is built on that assumption.

Lakhani's finding cracks the assumption. For breakthrough innovation work specifically — the kind of work where you need to generate options, evaluate them, synthesize across domains — an individual with the right AI support produces output equivalent to a small team without it. The capacity multiplier sits inside the augmented person, not inside the team headcount.

The unit of capacity is no longer the human. The unit of capacity is the augmented human.

Why this matters specifically for SMBs

Lakhani's study was conducted at P&G — a Fortune 500 enterprise. The translation to a 12-person business is sharper than people realize. Three reasons:

One: SMBs hire reluctantly. A 12-person business considering its 13th hire is making a much larger commitment, proportionally, than P&G considering its 5,000th. The asymmetric cost of a wrong hire is bigger. The asymmetric upside of avoiding a hire is bigger. Lakhani's finding gives SMBs a structural alternative: rather than the 13th hire, deploy an agent system that gives the existing 12 the leverage of 15. That's not a small adjustment to the org chart; it's a different way to scale.

Two: SMBs don't have organizational drag. Microsoft's Work Trend Index (2026) documents a finding adjacent to Lakhani's: middle managers slow-roll AI adoption when their identity is threatened. Enterprise rollouts are slow because the org structure resists. SMBs don't have middle managers — the founder is often the only decision-maker, and decisions get made fast. Whatever the Lakhani study showed about enterprise capacity gains, SMBs can capture more of, faster.

Three: the leverage compounds at small scale. A 10-person business that gains the equivalent of 5 augmented operators sees 50% capacity expansion. A 10,000-person business sees rounding error. The percentage gain from agent deployment is highest where the team is smallest. This inverts conventional wisdom that says AI is "more transformative" at enterprise scale; the opposite is true at the operating-impact level.

What to do on Monday morning

Reading the Lakhani paper and not changing anything is the failure mode most professionals fall into. The paper is not a vindication of buying more ChatGPT seats for your team. That's the trap.

The actionable version of Lakhani's finding is: redesign the capacity unit, not the tool stack. Don't ask "how can we use AI to make our work easier?" Ask "what work does our 12-person team currently struggle to keep up with, and could we deploy a standing agent — operating continuously, not just when a human prompts it — to own that work entirely?"

That second question is the question PRAGMA was built to answer. The Frontier Firm Operating System we install on client businesses is a specific operational translation of Lakhani's research: we deploy five to nine standing agents, each owning a specific operational function, working continuously rather than transactionally. The augmented capacity unit isn't a single human-with-AI; it's the operating layer above the humans, doing work no individual human had to take on.

The discovery phase of a PRAGMA engagement maps which functions in your business have this structure: high-volume, recurring, knowledge-shaped, currently consuming a disproportionate share of someone's time. Those functions are the candidates. The first agent deployment usually targets one of them. The system scales from there.
