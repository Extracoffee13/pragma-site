What actually happened

The HBS AI Institute, led by Professor Karim Lakhani, runs the Frontier Firm Initiative — an applied-research program studying what happens when companies organize around agentic AI instead of around traditional software. In March, they ran a working session with Microsoft. The premise was simple. Pull a group of operators into a room. Give them access to an agent-building platform. Ask them to ship every internal tool they'd been wanting for years but never had the budget to commission.

By the end of the afternoon, the room had produced 80 working internal applications. Tools that solved real, named problems inside real businesses. Not prototypes. Not concepts. Working software, in production, used by the people who built them.

What this means in plain English

Here is the version that matters to a business owner with a real payroll, a real lease, and a real bank line. For the last twenty years, the answer to "we need software for that" has been one of three options. Hire a developer for forty thousand a year. License a SaaS subscription for nine hundred a month forever. Or just don't have the tool, and have a person do it manually instead.

The HBS data point says all three of those options are now optional. A small group of operators, working with an agent-building system, can ship the equivalent of a dozen SaaS subscriptions in an afternoon. Custom to the business. Owned by the business. No monthly fee. No vendor lock-in. No "we don't support that feature in your tier."

The plumber's quoting tool. The landscaper's crew-scheduling board. The accountant's anomaly-flagging routine. The signage company's deck-rendering pipeline. The car dealership's lead-routing logic. The medical practice's intake summarization. Every one of those used to be a six-figure custom-software project or a four-figure-a-month SaaS bill. None of them require either anymore.

The cost of internal software has fallen to zero. The cost of knowing what to build has not.

Why the SMB owner hasn't heard about this yet

The HBS finding has been public since March. It's not classified. It's not a trade secret. The reason most SMB owners haven't heard about it is structural. The consulting firms whose income depends on billing hours have no incentive to tell a client that the project they were about to sell as a $250K engagement is now an afternoon's work. The SaaS vendors whose business model depends on perpetual subscriptions have no incentive to admit that the same functionality is now something a client can own outright.

HBR named this dynamic in its May 13 piece Beware the Agentic Convergence Trap. The argument runs in two directions. When competitors use the same tools, advantages erode. And when the established players sell tools, they have every reason to suppress the news that the tools no longer need to be sold.

The forthcoming HBR July–August cover, How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents, makes the same point about the incumbents being the slowest to admit it. The piece is not subtle. The thesis is that an agentic operating model "shrinks the time and capital needed to build companies" — and the incumbents who built their position on the old time and capital costs are now playing defense.

The mainstream business press has the story. It will reach the SMB owner's inbox in two waves. Wave one: business-school-trained operators read HBR, see the framing, and start applying it. Wave two: the same framing, dressed up by consulting firms, gets sold back to SMBs at a markup. PRAGMA exists in the gap between those two waves.
